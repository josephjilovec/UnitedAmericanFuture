Connected Communities Mobility Program

A useful transit network depends on the walk, roll, or ride that gets people to it. Connected Communities proposes small, place-specific studies of the gaps between homes, everyday destinations, and transit.

The question before the project

A trip is only as usable
as its hardest connection.

An improved bus service is less useful if a rider cannot reach the stop. A single missing sidewalk, difficult crossing, or obstructed boarding area can break an otherwise connected trip. The right unit of study is the whole route, with the people who actually use it.

Who a first study is for
A municipality, transit agency, metropolitan planning organization, or community partner working with the responsible road authority.

Neighborhood mobility / closer look

Access is a continuous
experience.

A crossing, shelter, and boarding area can each look good in isolation while the journey between them remains difficult. The study records those breaks and who has authority to resolve them.

People with disabilities should help shape the question and evaluation. Their participation supports, rather than replaces, professional accessibility review.

A practical scale

One route.
Clear responsibilities.

Start small enough that a public owner can act on the findings. The first brief should identify maintenance fixes, design questions, and larger projects separately, including who would fund, approve, and maintain each one.

The aim is a usable journey, not a collection of disconnected street features.

A concrete first step

Neighborhood Access Pilot Brief

Proposed scope: one bounded route or station catchment agreed with its public owner. A commissioned study would need a named owner, qualified reviewers, agreed deliverables, real quotes, and a capped budget before work begins.

01

Route and barrier record

A defined catchment, ownership map, existing plans, user observations, and evidence gaps across the complete journey.

02

Options with tradeoffs

Compare maintenance, access repairs, crossing improvements, curb changes, and larger redesigns. Include transit operations and emergency access.

03

Owner-ready pilot brief

A proposed scope, professional design questions, accessibility review, permission pathway, maintenance owner, and evaluation plan.

This is a proposed deliverable, not an available funded engagement or an announcement of a selected location. Fees, eligibility, and delivery dates would be set only for an agreed scope.

How the proposal advances

Evidence before
the next commitment.

A study may recommend a smaller intervention, further research, or no project. Each is a useful outcome when the reasoning is clear.

  1. 01

    Choose the everyday trip

    Bound the route around a specific destination and willing public owner.

  2. 02

    Walk and review the evidence

    Use existing records first; arrange any field observations with permissions and an accessible engagement plan.

  3. 03

    Resolve the broken links

    Compare route continuity, crossing exposure, loading, transit reliability, and maintenance—not isolated amenities.

  4. 04

    Hand over an implementable brief

    The responsible authority accepts the study or requests changes before any design or temporary installation.

Before we begin

A few practical
questions.

Will UAF redesign or control a street?+

The proposed first work is an access study. The road authority and qualified designers retain responsibility for engineering, traffic operations, approvals, and any construction.

How does this connect to Iron Corridor?+

Iron Corridor studies intercity rail. Connected Communities studies the everyday connections around a neighborhood or station. They can inform each other, but each place needs its own owner, evidence, and scope.

Does a demonstration mean installing cones or signals?+

No installation is authorized by this concept. Any temporary change needs the responsible authority, a reviewed design and traffic-control plan, insurance, and an agreed removal and maintenance process.
