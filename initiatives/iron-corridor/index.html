Public-interest infrastructure planning

A national vision.
Regional decisions.

The useful starting point is not a promise to build thousands of miles of railway. It is a disciplined way to find the corridors worth studying.

Iron Corridor brings passenger rail research into United American Future's wider mission of opportunity, community strength, skilled work, and long-term national capacity.

Its first task is to compare travel needs, existing and proposed rail services, engineering constraints, local priorities, and potential public-sector sponsors. Each corridor must make its own case before becoming part of a larger network.

A concept, not an approved construction program.

No route alignment, station site, operating speed, project cost, public award, construction date, or delivery partnership is established by this proposal.

The United American High-Speed Rail Network

Three potential hubs.
Five corridor families.

The starting architecture explores Denver, Chicago, and Houston. Their roles are questions to test, not fixed conclusions. Passenger demand, terrain, travel time, connections, and operating requirements may change the network substantially.

Potential hub / Mountain West

Denver

Investigate Front Range and wider Mountain West connections. Test whether Denver serves best as a transfer hub or regional gateway, especially where mountain terrain changes cost and journey time.

Potential hub / Midwest

Chicago

Examine Midwest and Great Lakes city pairs, station interfaces, and connections with existing services. A useful interchange depends on practical timetables and capacity, not its place on a map.

Potential hub / Texas & Gulf

Houston

Explore Texas travel markets and Gulf Coast connections. Compare regional service options, existing development efforts, land needs, resilience, and a credible public-sector sponsor.

Candidate markets, not approved routes

Explore the corridor families.

These groupings organize research. They do not set a construction sequence or imply that every connection would justify high-speed infrastructure.

01 / Pacific+

Markets to investigate: Seattle, Portland, San Francisco, Los Angeles, San Diego.

Question: Which regional city pairs add useful service alongside existing and proposed West Coast rail, with practical station access and a defensible operating model?

02 / Mountain+

Markets to investigate: Denver, the Front Range, Salt Lake City, Phoenix, Las Vegas.

Question: Where does demand justify new infrastructure once terrain, distance, construction risk, power supply, and alternatives are included?

03 / Central+

Markets to investigate: Denver, Chicago, Kansas City, St. Louis, Dallas/Fort Worth, Houston, Austin, San Antonio.

Question: Which regional links can stand on their own and later connect reliably, rather than relying on a speculative coast-to-coast journey?

04 / Gulf & Southeast+

Markets to investigate: Houston, New Orleans, Atlanta, Jacksonville, Miami, and connections toward the Mid-Atlantic.

Question: How do regional travel demand, flood and storm resilience, station access, and public sponsorship shape useful service options?

05 / Northeast & Great Lakes+

Markets to investigate: Chicago, Minneapolis, Detroit, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Washington, New York, Boston.

Question: Where could new or improved service complement existing corridors, relieve specific bottlenecks, and connect passengers without incompatible infrastructure?

The map is a simplified illustration of selected connections. The market lists describe a broader research scope. International extensions would require their own sponsors, agreements, and analysis.

Compare city-pair demand, existing services, fares, frequency, reliability, and door-to-door access.

Screen terrain, existing rights-of-way, stations, land access, utilities, freight interfaces, and power.

Study access, community effects, safety, environmental tradeoffs, and station-area connections.

Development in stages

A pathway to study.
Then a case to develop.

Progress depends on governance, expertise, permission, funding, and the findings at each stage. This is not a construction timetable.

  1. 01 / Current

    Define the network question.

    Publish the concept, compare it with existing plans, and identify the information needed to test it.

  2. 02 / Proposed next work

    Screen regional corridors.

    Use comparable demand, access, engineering, cost, public-benefit, and sponsorship criteria. Document the gaps.

  3. 03 / Subject to partners & resources

    Develop a sponsored study.

    Seek an eligible sponsor and qualified advisers to define alternatives, feasibility work, study costs, and the appropriate review process.

  4. 04 / A separate future decision

    Advance an implementable project.

    Responsible agencies and qualified delivery partners would need their own approvals, finance, land rights, designs, procurement, safety arrangements, and operating plan.

A few clear answers

What this proposal means.

Is this an approved high-speed rail project?+

No. It is a public-interest planning concept. The map does not establish land rights, an alignment, a selected technology, project funding, or permission to build.

Are Denver, Chicago, and Houston final hubs?+

No. They are the starting architecture to test. A more useful regional network, different interchange, or decision not to pursue a connection may emerge from independent work.

Will every line be high-speed rail?+

That is not determined. High-speed, improved intercity service, and connections with other modes need to be compared corridor by corridor. Service speeds, train technology, compatibility, and infrastructure are study questions.

Is United American Future building or operating a railway?+

Not at this stage. The proposed UAF role is research, public-interest planning, convening, and partner development. Construction and operations would require capable and authorized entities, separate agreements, and the necessary approvals.

Does this replace the other UAF initiatives?+

No. Iron Corridor is a featured national-capacity initiative within UAF's wider civic mission. Industry, community mobility, aerial logistics, water, and conservation remain distinct areas that require their own evidence and partners.
